Signal extraction

Turning a single free-text response — in English, Hindi, or the code-mixed Hindi-English people actually speak — into three calibrated numbers: how pleasant or unpleasant the speaker feels, how activated they are, and how much they hedge. Built as a measurable instrument, not a sentiment score.

Most sentiment tooling returns a label and no way to check it. This returns numbers on a named psychological model, against a written schema, and reports its own accuracy — including where it is weak and where it fails outright.

30 responses rated independently by 2 human raters (Ashwin Srivastava and Prashant); every response was scored by three methods — the language model glm-5.2, Jev typesafe-ai/jev, and the lexicon control nrc-vad-1. Each model correlation is shown against the inter-rater ceiling — how well the two humans agree with each other on that axis. Where the ceiling is low, the axis is not measurable at this sample size whatever the model scores, and it says so below.

What is measured

Valence and arousal are the two axes of Russell's Circumplex Model of Affect (Russell, 1980), scored independently — a calm, content response is pleasant with low arousal; fury is unpleasant with high arousal. Hedging is measured separately as qualifying language in the response itself.

How it works

Grounded, not vibesBoth axes are defined by a named, citable model before any scoring happens, so "how positive is this" is not the model's private judgment.
Structured output, forcedThe model must return the schema via a forced tool call. Nothing parses free text, so a malformed score cannot silently pass.
Code-mixed, nativelyHindi, English and Hinglish are scored as written. No translation step — translation is where tone is destroyed.

Three scoring methods, run in parallel

Every response is scored by each of these, and the harness measures all three against the same human ratings. A method that failed on an item is reported as a failure for that item, never imputed, so a partial pass is visible above rather than smoothed away.

Why three. Not to crown a winner up front: the point is to let this harness decide which method is accurate enough to trust at scale, because a cheaper or faster method only matters once its accuracy clears the bar. On this sample the two model-based methods are not separable: valence reads +0.96 for the language model against +0.96 for Jev, and arousal +0.49 against +0.54, with the 95% intervals overlapping on both. The language model is ahead on hedging (+0.81 against +0.70), which is the axis whose human ceiling is lowest, so neither number settles much. The defensible claim is parity on the two axes where the raters agree, not a win: at this sample size the intervals cannot separate 0.96 from 0.96.

Read Jev with its caveats attached. It is a typed-decision model, not a reasoning model, so a schema-valid score is not automatically a correct one — the correlations above are the only evidence that it can be trusted, and nothing here justifies using it beyond triage without them. Its own vendor benchmark reports roughly 68% accuracy on their internal workflow evals: a starting expectation, not a guarantee for this task. It scores by anchor probability, so its scale is stepped at about 0.05 rather than continuous, which caps how high its r can climb at this sample size. And the gateway key observed a five-requests-per-minute limit, which is why the harness paces its calls at 12 s and retries transient failures instead of assuming one call per response.

The ceiling is the number that makes each model r interpretable. A model cannot be judged against an axis the raters themselves score differently, and a model r that exceeds a low ceiling is not evidence of skill — it is evidence that the axis has no stable human answer to hit. With 2 raters there is a single pair, so this ceiling is itself a noisy estimate; three or four raters would tighten it.

What this does not prove yet

  • Two raters is the minimum for a ceiling, not the target. The language model's valence now reads +0.96 and Jev's +0.96, against a ceiling of +0.88, which is a defensible number for this module. Every other axis needs more raters before its ceiling stops being one noisy pair.
  • Hedging has no usable human ground truth here. The raters agree at +0.31 — below the 0.50 threshold — so the model's +0.81 on that axis is not evidence it can measure hedging. It is evidence that "how much does this person hedge" is being read differently by different humans. Fixing the rubric is the next task, not tuning the model.
  • Arousal is the weak axis, and now that is measurable. +0.49 against a ceiling of +0.71: the model sits clearly below what the humans can agree on, so this is model error rather than ambiguity in the measure.
  • Jev is a triage instrument until its numbers say otherwise. It is a typed-decision model, not a reasoning model, so a schema-valid score is not automatically a correct one. On this sample its valence and arousal sit level with the language model's (+0.96 and +0.54 against +0.96 and +0.49) — enough to justify a cheap first pass at scale, not enough to replace the language model where the decision matters. Its own vendor benchmark reports roughly 68% on their internal evals, and its anchor-based scale is stepped rather than continuous; both facts hold its numbers down, and neither is a reason to trust it further than the correlations above.
  • A model r above the ceiling is not proof. The model is correlated against the mean of the ratings, which is more reliable than either single rater, while the ceiling is computed between single raters. The two numbers are therefore not strictly comparable, and the ceiling is a floor on the axis's reliability, not a target the model has beaten.
  • The model is not deterministic. Two runs over the same 30 responses differed on roughly half the items (mean absolute difference 0.06 on valence, up to 0.50 on hedging), moving the reported correlation by about 0.05. Valence was stable; averaging repeated samples is the fix.
  • The lexicon cannot ship. NRC-VAD is licensed for research use; commercial use requires written consent from NRC, and redistribution is prohibited. It is used here as a research control only.
  • This validates a module, not a business. The claim above is about scoring one response. It says nothing yet about scale, cost per response, or behaviour on real deployment traffic.

Why it matters

Any team can call a model and print a sentiment score. The defensible asset is the discipline around it: a fixed schema, a citable psychological model, two further methods as controls, a blind rating pack scored by more than one person, a measured result with its ceiling attached, and a written account of where it fails. That is what makes a capability claim auditable — by a client, a partner, or an investor's technical advisor. The same harness is what will train a domain-specific model later, because it already produces labelled data in a fixed format.
