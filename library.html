Pulse Library · base intelligence

Before you pay for 800 interviews, find out how many you already have.

Every JanPulse study feeds a corpus that answers the next study’s questions first. Pulse Library is that corpus: a coverage report before fieldwork is commissioned, a consensus and divergence view that is provably disjoint, and a citation trail from every claim back to the response that supports it.

This page is a working demonstration of the interface and the statistics on a synthetic dataset. The live library is populated by real fieldwork; the structure below does not change.

Step 02 · Consensus and divergence

What this population agrees on, and what divides it

Two lists, computed with Laplace-smoothed proportions and a one-proportion z-test at 90% confidence. Because the two probabilities sum to at most one, a statement cannot appear in both lists — that is a proof, not a filter.

Each statement is then tested again inside every opinion group. A statement carried by one large bloc is not consensus, however large its overall majority; only statements that clear the same threshold in both groups are marked as bridging.

      Step 03 · Opinion structure

      Two opinion groups, not one average

      Two-level clustering over a sparsity-weighted PCA projection, with the group count chosen by silhouette

      Where the groups part company

      Polarising score = |correlation| between an item’s answers and a respondent’s position on the main axis of opinion. It is a property of the statement, not of a group; near 0 means the item does not separate anyone.

      Step 04 · The citation trail

      Every claim, back to the response

      The part most research tools leave out

      What a real answer carries

      • Evidence references — study, item, response and turn identifiers, so a reviewer can open the transcript.
      • Consent scope — whether the record may be reused in pooled analysis, only in the client deliverable, or not at all.
      • Reliability — the inter-rater ceiling and the scorer’s measured agreement on held-out data, with sample size. If a scorer has no measured figure, any claim resting on it is marked thin, not supported.
      • Coverage — how many records matched, how many were excluded by consent or access, and what the corpus could not answer.
      What is synthetic here

      The numbers on this page are illustrative. The method is not.

      Synthetic

      • The 360 respondents and every percentage and count derived from them.
      • The three quoted responses, including the Marathi and Hindi text.
      • The prior-wave sizes and evidence ages behind the coverage table.

      Real

      • The statistics: Laplace-smoothed proportions, one-proportion z-tests at z90 = 1.2816, silhouette-based group selection, two-level k-means on a sparsity-weighted projection.
      • The disjointness property of the consensus lists.
      • The citation structure and the reliability fields, which exist in the data model now and are populated by measured figures when real data lands.

      Limits we state up front

      • Below roughly 30 respondents per cohort the clustering layer is noise; such cells are suppressed, not published.
      • The main axis has an arbitrary sign and near-ties in variance. Direction is never presented as meaning — only relative position.
      • Consensus describes agreement with statements. It says nothing about whether a policy is good, and we do not let a report imply otherwise.
      • Everything here is conditional on the instrument. A badly worded item produces a clean, significant, meaningless consensus — which is why the instrument review gate exists.
