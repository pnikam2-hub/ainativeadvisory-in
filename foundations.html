Our foundations · Livelihood Intelligence

Making the invisible visible. Making the inaudible heard.

Every organisation has an invisible population. For governments, it may be informal workers, home-based entrepreneurs or citizens absent from administrative records. For businesses, it may be employees whose real concerns never reach leadership, consumers who never fill out feedback forms, or potential customers who never appear in a CRM.

Traditional systems capture what is registered, reported and volunteered. JanPulse builds on population-intelligence methodologies developed for public-sector initiatives in Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh, designed to discover the realities those systems miss.

The intelligence of an organisation is limited not by how much data it possesses, but by how much of its underlying reality it can understand.

Case study 01 · Public intelligence · Maharashtra

Mumbai Suburban Skill Census

Understanding aspirations that conventional data could not see

A qualified young person may be working in an unrelated gig-economy role. Administrative records might capture education, occupation and residence, yet miss aspirations, barriers, nearby employment demand and the specific pathway that could improve the person’s livelihood. Multiply that gap across thousands of households and it becomes an institutional intelligence problem.

The Mumbai Suburban exercise sought to close that gap: not by adding another static report, but by treating the survey as the beginning of a continuously improving intelligence system.

The AI-native approach

  • Adaptive questionnaires — follow-up questions tailored to each respondent’s occupation, aspirations and circumstances rather than a single rigid form.
  • Multimodal listening — structured responses combined, where appropriate and consented, with natural-language descriptions, voice, images and field observations.
  • Contextual inference — linking individual observations with local training, employer demand, household circumstances and geography; distinguishing observed facts from model-derived estimates.
  • Response quality signals — identifying inconsistent or incomplete responses and triggering clarification or follow-up.
  • Continuity — designing for later updates through outreach, digital channels and callbacks instead of treating the census as a one-time snapshot.

From population signals to a Public Knowledge Brain

Population signals could be connected with government schemes, administrative knowledge, training institutions and employer information in a governed, permissioned knowledge environment. The resulting queryable foundation could support questions such as: which wards have both latent skills and unmet employer demand? Which groups are not reached by existing programmes? Where should livelihood interventions be concentrated? AI supported interpretation; authorised officials retained decisions.

Why this matters for JanPulse. Enterprises face the same blind spots. Headcount, engagement scores and attrition dashboards rarely explain hidden aspirations, team-level barriers or why certain groups remain unheard. JanPulse can adapt the underlying method to listen across teams, branches, sites and workforce segments, combine those signals with organisational context, and help leaders understand what is happening, why, and where intervention is needed.

Case study 02 · Economic intelligence · Uttar Pradesh

Uttar Pradesh Unorganised Economy Census

Revealing the economy missing from establishment records

Across Lucknow, Gorakhpur, Meerut and Varanasi, the initiative examined livelihoods that traditional establishment-centric economic statistics often underrepresent: household trading, home-based production, informal services and mixed or seasonal work. Two people labelled with the same occupation could face very different customer networks, costs, supply chains and credit needs.

The AI-native approach

  • Household-first discovery — recording multiple economic activities within a household rather than looking only for registered establishments.
  • Hyperlocal context — relating trade performance to neighbourhood clusters, transport access, suppliers, customer demand and seasonality.
  • Rich economic profiles — capturing what is made or sold, quantities, prices, equipment, dependencies, distances and income patterns.
  • Derived intelligence — using statistical and machine-learning methods to estimate working-capital cycles, seasonality and potential credit needs, with appropriate uncertainty.
  • Permissioned knowledge — controlling access to sensitive information and restricting uses to authorised purposes.

From invisible activity to actionable economic intelligence

The book describes how such granular information could differentiate livelihoods needing credit from those needing market access or cluster-level intervention. It also discusses bottom-up district economic measurement through the concept of District Domestic Product. The essential breakthrough was not a larger dataset; it was the ability to ask questions about economic behaviour at the level where that behaviour actually occurs.

Why this matters for JanPulse. A business may understand its existing customers through sales and digital analytics, while knowing little about non-buyers, underserved groups, lost customers or people outside its distribution footprint. JanPulse can use contextual, targeted listening to uncover demand and barriers that transaction records cannot reveal, helping organisations improve products, routes to market, customer experience and expansion decisions.

The evolution

From a Public Knowledge Brain to an Organisational Intelligence Brain

Mumbai demonstrated how to understand aspirations, capabilities and barriers. Uttar Pradesh demonstrated how to understand hidden economic activity and the local relationships shaping behaviour. Together they form a methodological pedigree for JanPulse: a system for discovering missing voices, combining them with context, assessing confidence and turning signals into decision intelligence.
